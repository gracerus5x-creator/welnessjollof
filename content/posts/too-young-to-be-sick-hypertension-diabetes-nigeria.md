"I'm young, I feel fine." That single thought is how high blood pressure and
diabetes catch so many Nigerians in their 20s and 30s — quietly, with no
warning, until the damage is already done. These conditions are no longer "an
old person's problem." This guide covers the numbers that matter and the simple
steps that lower your risk.

## They're common — and they're getting younger

Roughly **one in three Nigerian adults** now lives with high blood pressure, and
**more than 11 million** live with diabetes. In 2024 alone, diabetes was linked
to **over 55,000 deaths** in Nigeria. These are not rare, far-away statistics —
they are family, colleagues, and increasingly, young adults.

## Why they're called "silent" killers

The danger isn't just how common they are — it's how quietly they work. **Fewer
than 3 in 10** people with hypertension are even aware they have it. For
diabetes it's worse: across the IDF Africa Region, **54% of people living with
diabetes are undiagnosed** — the highest of any region in the world.

You can feel completely well while your blood pressure or blood sugar is doing
damage in the background. "Feeling fine" is not the same as being healthy.

## Know your numbers

The only way to catch a silent killer is to measure it.

- **Blood pressure** is generally considered high at **140/90 or above.** A check
  takes two minutes at any pharmacy or clinic.
- **Blood sugar** has its own cut-offs — but those are for your clinician to read
  and explain, not to self-judge from a single reading.

If you don't know your numbers, that's the first thing to fix. Nigeria's
**"Know Your Numbers"** screening drive (part of the national *Project 10
Million*) exists precisely so people can check for free at hundreds of sites.

## What untreated damage looks like

Left unmanaged, high blood pressure and diabetes are leading causes of
**stroke, heart attack, kidney failure, vision loss, and amputation.** The point
of catching them early isn't fear — it's that early, they're very manageable;
late, they take your health, your savings, or both.

## The money reason to catch it early

Managing these conditions in Nigeria has become brutally expensive. Reporting in
2026 puts the rise in **insulin prices at more than 200% since 2023** — a vial
that cost about **₦3,500 now runs ₦18,000–₦21,000**, and a pack of five
long-acting pens went from around **₦22,000 to ₦58,000.** Some patients now
spend **over ₦100,000 a month** on diabetes care.

With most health costs paid out of pocket, that price tag pushes people into
rationing or stopping treatment — which is exactly how complications begin.
Catching trouble early, while lifestyle still does much of the work, is the
cheapest medicine there is.

## How to lower your risk

None of this guarantees anything — but each step meaningfully lowers your risk:

1. **Know your numbers.** Get your blood pressure and blood sugar checked, even if
   you feel fine — especially if hypertension or diabetes runs in your family.
2. **Ease off the salt.** The WHO recommends adults keep salt **under 5 g a day**
   — roughly one level teaspoon, counting what's already in seasoning cubes,
   processed food, and the pot.
3. **Move most days.** Aim for the WHO's **150 minutes of moderate activity a
   week** — brisk walking counts.
4. **Lean into whole foods,** ease off sugary drinks and heavily processed food.
5. **Family history matters.** If a close relative has hypertension or diabetes,
   your risk is higher — so check earlier and more often.

## Get checked

You don't need to feel sick to be at risk. Book a blood-pressure and blood-sugar
check this month — at your pharmacy, clinic, or a free screening site. Knowing
your numbers is the single most powerful thing you can do in your 20s and 30s.
